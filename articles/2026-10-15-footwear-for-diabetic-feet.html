Choosing the Right Footwear for Diabetic Feet | Diabetic Foot Care Karimnagar

15 October 2026 · 4 min read

For a person with diabetes, the wrong pair of shoes can do real harm. Choosing the right footwear is one of the simplest and most effective parts of diabetic foot care, and our team at Dhruva Foot Health Clinic in Karimnagar discusses it with patients every week. Here is what makes a shoe safe for diabetic feet — and which styles to avoid.

Why footwear matters so much for diabetic feet

Many diabetic foot ulcers begin with minor trauma from footwear: a blister from a tight shoe, a rub from a rough seam, or pressure from a pointed toe. In a person with diabetic neuropathy, these injuries may go completely unfelt, so the shoe keeps rubbing and the blister deepens. Poor circulation then slows healing, and a small break in the skin can turn into an ulcer needing professional treatment. The right shoes remove this risk at the source by spreading pressure evenly, preventing rubbing, and protecting feet from knocks and foreign objects.

What to look for in safe footwear

  • A wide, deep toe box — toes should wiggle freely; no part of the shoe should press on toes or toenails.
  • Adjustable fastening — laces or Velcro straps let you adjust the fit as feet swell through the day.
  • Thick, cushioned soles — these absorb shock and reduce pressure on the sole of the foot.
  • Breathable uppers — soft leather or mesh keeps feet drier and lowers the risk of fungal infections.
  • Low, broad heels — high or narrow heels concentrate pressure on the forefoot and should be avoided.
  • Smooth interiors — no raised seams, rough edges or loose labels that could rub the skin.

Styles to avoid

  • High heels and pointed shoes — they squeeze the toes and raise pressure on the ball of the foot.
  • Flip-flops and thong sandals — no protection, and the strap between the toes causes friction.
  • Plastic or rubber shoes — they trap moisture against the skin, encouraging fungal infections.
  • Shoes that hurt when new — never "break in" a painful shoe by force; if it pinches in the shop, it will injure at home.
  • Going barefoot — never walk barefoot, even indoors; a protective shoe or slipper is always safer.

Buying and wearing new shoes safely

  • Shop in the afternoon, when feet are slightly swollen, so the fit works all day.
  • Wear the socks you normally use, and have both feet measured — one foot is often slightly larger.
  • Inspect the inside of each shoe before wearing it: feel for seams, labels or small objects.
  • Wear new shoes for short periods at first and check your feet afterwards for redness or pressure marks.
  • Consider seamless, moisture-wicking diabetic socks for extra protection against friction.

When to see a foot specialist

See a foot specialist if shoes that once fit well now cause blisters, if pressure marks keep recurring, or if changes in foot shape make comfortable shoes hard to find. A professional foot assessment can identify pressure points before they become wounds.
