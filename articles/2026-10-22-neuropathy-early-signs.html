Diabetic Neuropathy: Early Warning Signs in Your Feet

22 October 2026 · 4 min read

తెలుగులో చదవండి

Diabetic neuropathy — nerve damage caused by long-term high blood sugar — is the most common complication of diabetes, and it usually shows up in the feet first. Spotting the early signs of diabetic neuropathy and acting on them is one of the best ways to protect your feet from serious problems such as ulcers and infections.

What diabetic neuropathy does to your feet

Over time, high blood sugar damages the small nerves that carry feeling to and from your feet. Because the nerves to the feet are the longest in the body, they are affected first. This is called peripheral neuropathy. In many people only sensation is affected; in others, the nerves that control small foot muscles weaken too, which can change how weight falls on the foot.

Neuropathy can develop silently for years. By the time a person notices something is wrong, the feet may already be losing protective sensation — which is why knowing the early signs matters.

Early signs of diabetic neuropathy to watch for

  • Tingling or "pins and needles" in the toes or feet, often worse at night.
  • Burning or aching pain in the feet, even when you are resting.
  • Numbness — the feet feel "asleep" or you cannot tell whether bathwater is hot or cold.
  • Sharp, stabbing or electric-shock-like pains that come and go.
  • Extreme sensitivity — even the touch of a bedsheet feels painful (doctors call this allodynia).
  • Cramp-like pains in the feet or calves.
  • Unsteadiness or loss of balance, especially in the dark, because the feet no longer "report" the ground properly.

Symptoms usually start in the toes and creep upward in a "stocking" pattern, and they almost always affect both feet. If symptoms are only in one foot, or begin suddenly, tell your doctor — that can point to a different cause.

Why early signs matter: the silent danger

The real danger of neuropathy is not the discomfort — it is the loss of warning signals. A foot that cannot feel pain cannot warn you about a blister rubbing inside a shoe, a thorn in the sole, or bathwater that is too hot. These small, unnoticed injuries are the most common starting point of diabetic foot ulcers.

Worse, many people with diabetes also have reduced blood flow to the feet, so once an injury does occur, it heals slowly and can become infected. Diabetic foot ulcer treatment is far simpler, shorter and more successful when the ulcer is caught early — which is exactly what recognising neuropathy symptoms makes possible.

What to do if you notice these signs

  • Do not dismiss tingling or numbness as "just age" or tiredness — report it to your doctor or a foot specialist such as Dr Sreekar Sura at Dhruva Foot Health Clinic, Karimnagar.
  • Note when the symptoms started, which parts of the foot they affect, and whether both feet are involved; this helps the doctor assess the pattern.
  • A foot specialist can test your sensation with simple, painless checks — for example, touching the foot with a thin filament or testing vibration sense with a tuning fork.
  • Ask how often your feet should be checked from now on; people with neuropathy need more frequent foot examinations.

Protecting your nerves and feet long term

  • Keep your blood sugar in its target range — this is the single most important step in slowing nerve damage.
  • Check both feet every day for cuts, blisters, redness or swelling, since your nerves may no longer warn you.
  • Never walk barefoot, indoors or outdoors, and wear well-fitting footwear with no hard seams.
  • Test bathwater with your elbow or a thermometer, not your foot.
  • Report any new or worsening symptom promptly — early diabetic neuropathy treatment and foot care can prevent small problems from becoming serious ones.
