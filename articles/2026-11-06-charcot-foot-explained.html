Charcot Foot Explained: Diabetic Foot Care in Karimnagar

6 November 2026 · 4 min read

తెలుగులో చదవండి

Charcot foot is a serious complication of diabetes that weakens the bones of the foot. It often begins quietly — with swelling and warmth, and little or no pain — so it is easy to miss. Understanding it early matters, because timely care can protect the shape of the foot and help a person keep walking. This article explains what it is, what causes it, and what to watch for, as part of diabetic foot care in Karimnagar.

What is Charcot foot?

Charcot foot (also called Charcot neuroarthropathy) is a condition in which the bones, joints and soft tissues of the foot weaken and break down. Over weeks or months, the bones can fracture and the joints can collapse, and the shape of the foot changes — often the arch falls, and the middle of the foot becomes flat or rocker-shaped. It usually affects one foot.

Why it happens in diabetes

Charcot foot is closely linked to diabetic neuropathy — nerve damage that reduces feeling in the feet. When feeling is reduced, a person may not notice a small injury, and may keep walking on a foot that is already injured. Repeated stress on weakened bones, together with increased blood flow that can soften bone, leads to fractures and joint damage. It most often develops in people who have had diabetes for many years.

Warning signs to watch for

Because pain is often absent, the early signs are easy to overlook. Compare both feet regularly and look for:

  • Swelling in one foot — often around the middle of the foot, with the other foot normal.
  • Warmth and redness in one foot but not the other.
  • Little or no pain, even when the foot looks swollen.
  • A change in the shape of the foot, such as a flattening arch or a bulge on the sole.
  • A shoe that used to fit no longer fits on one side.

Swelling and warmth can also be signs of infection, which needs urgent attention — so a hot, swollen foot in a person with diabetes should always be checked the same day.

How it is diagnosed and treated

Diagnosis starts with an examination and a comparison of both feet, followed by X-rays and sometimes an MRI scan. A key part of the assessment is ruling out infection, because an infected foot and a Charcot foot can look similar at first. The main treatment is rest and offloading — keeping weight off the affected foot, often with a cast or a removable boot, for several weeks or months while the bones settle. After that, specially made footwear helps protect the foot. Surgery is sometimes needed when the foot has changed shape a great deal, or when it is unstable. Early treatment can often prevent permanent deformity and the ulcers that follow it — which is why wound care in Karimnagar begins with recognising the problem early.

How to protect your feet

  • Check both feet every day, and compare them — swelling or warmth in one foot is worth attention.
  • Never ignore a swollen, warm foot; get it checked the same day, even if it does not hurt.
  • Wear protective, well-fitting footwear and never walk barefoot.
  • If you have been told you have neuropathy, take extra care: reduced feeling means injuries can go unnoticed.
