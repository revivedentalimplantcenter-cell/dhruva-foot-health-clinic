Diabetic Foot: What It Is and Why It Needs Special Care

13 October 2026 · 4 min read

తెలుగులో చదవండి

"Diabetic foot" is the term doctors use for the foot problems that develop as a complication of diabetes. It is one of the most common — and most preventable — complications of the condition. Understanding what happens to the feet in diabetes is the first step to protecting them.

How diabetes harms the feet

Long-term high blood sugar damages the body in three ways that come together in the feet:

  • Nerve damage (neuropathy) — reduces feeling, so injuries, pressure and heat go unnoticed.
  • Poor blood circulation — less blood reaches the feet, so wounds heal slowly and infections take hold more easily.
  • Weakened immunity in the wound area — small breaks in the skin can turn into deep infections faster than in people without diabetes.

Because of this combination, a blister from a tight shoe — something trivial for most people — can become a serious ulcer in a person with diabetes.

Common diabetic foot problems

  • Foot ulcers — open sores, most often on the sole or toes, that need professional care.
  • Infections — redness, swelling, warmth or discharge around any break in the skin.
  • Corns, callus and cracked heels — thickened skin that can break down into ulcers under pressure.
  • Changes in foot shape — long-term nerve and joint changes can alter how weight falls on the foot.
  • Numbness, tingling or burning — early signs of neuropathy worth getting checked.

Protecting your feet: the basics

  • Check both feet every day for cuts, blisters, redness or swelling.
  • Never walk barefoot — indoors or outdoors.
  • Wear well-fitting, comfortable footwear and inspect the insides of shoes before wearing them.
  • Wash feet daily, dry gently (especially between the toes), and moisturise the feet — avoiding between the toes.
  • Keep blood sugar under control; it is the single biggest factor in preventing complications.
  • Have your feet examined by a specialist at least once a year, even if nothing hurts.

When to seek care early

See a foot specialist promptly if you notice a sore that is not improving, spreading redness, swelling, discharge, new numbness, or any change in foot shape or skin colour. With diabetic foot problems, early treatment is simpler, shorter and far more effective than late treatment.

This article is for general information only and is not a substitute for medical advice. If you have a foot wound or worrying symptoms, please consult a doctor.
